What are esports data APIs?

An esports data API is a programmatic feed of competitive gaming information: fixtures, live scores, rosters, player and team statistics, and in some products odds and settlement. Buyers include media sites, fantasy products, sportsbooks and developers building companion apps. Sources range from official publisher data to community parsers and commercial aggregators.

How we ranked these

Anyone building a schedule widget, a stats site or a betting product asks the same two questions: where does the data come from, and what does a request cost? The ten candidates on this list answer differently.

Some are commercial feeds sold on quote to sportsbooks, one publishes self-serve prices, a few are free but limited to a single game, and one is a developer gateway into a single publisher's titles. We read vendor pages, documentation indexes and pricing tables, noted what each one says about coverage and licensing, and ranked by how well a small to mid-size product team could actually start.

Official and odds-grade sources rank above single-game hobby feeds when the facts support it.

Title coverage and depth

Count the games and the layer of detail. A schedule feed that lists results is a different product from one with 350 data points per match. Vendors here range from five flagship titles to a dozen and more, and a few go no further than one game.

Check that your titles are named, not implied.

Data provenance and licensing

Official data from a publisher or league carries rights that scraped statistics do not. Look for the words official, licensed or integrity in the vendor copy, and read restrictions on use: one vendor bars betting-related usage from its stats plans, while others sell only to operators.

Access model and cost

Self-serve keys with published rate limits suit a prototype; quote-only contracts suit a sportsbook. Ask whether a free tier or trial exists, how rate limits are counted, and whether a per-game fee applies. Hidden pricing is common in this niche, so plan a sales call into your timeline.

Delivery and tooling

REST and GraphQL are the norm, but push delivery over a socket matters for live odds. Widgets, white-label sites and documentation quality decide how fast a small team ships. Check for an interactive docs site and for helpers such as champion, hero or map lookups.

What this list shows

Pricing transparency is rare here. Of ten products, PandaScore alone prints figures (stats plans from 400 euros and 1000 euros per game per month, with a free tier at 1000 requests per hour). Five more, GRID, Oddin.gg, Sportradar, GameScorekeeper and OddsMatrix, sell on quote or demo, with OddsMatrix adding a one-month trial.

OpenDota is open source, STRATZ is free with a paid STRATZ+ tier, and Riot and Steamworks gate access through applications.

Geography skews European among vendors that name a base: PandaScore in Paris, GRID in Berlin, GameScorekeeper in Aarhus and Sportradar in St. Gallen. The trade-off is between licensed breadth and open access. Commercial feeds give rights, uptime promises and several titles but need a contract, while community feeds cost nothing and cover one game.

Teams building for betting should check license terms first, because the cheaper plans often exclude that use.

How to choose

1. Pin down the use case and licensing

Decide whether you are showing scores, powering fantasy points or pricing odds. Betting use is the strictest: one self-serve vendor excludes it from its stats plans, and operator-focused vendors sell only through sales. Write the use case into your first email so you are quoted the right product.

2. List the titles and the data depth you need

Write down the games and the fields, such as live round data, player stats or only results. Compare against the coverage pages. A vendor with twelve titles and results may still lack the per-round fields your widget needs, and a single-game parser may be deeper than any commercial feed.

3. Prototype on a free key before you sign

Use PandaScore's free plan, OpenDota, STRATZ or a Riot developer key to test your schema, then ask a commercial vendor for a trial. Check documentation quality, rate limits and push delivery during the trial, not after the contract.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best esports data API in 2026?

PandaScore is first for most teams: it has a free tier, published stats plans, and covers League of Legends, CS2, Dota 2 and more. GRID is the better pick when official data and a sportsbook-grade contract matter, and GameScorekeeper is stronger when you need a dozen titles through a documented REST API.

Which esports data providers offer official data?

GRID describes itself as an official esports live data platform covering five titles. Oddin.gg offers an official data feed for operators. Riot's developer portal is the publisher's own gateway to its games.

Ask each about the leagues and rights behind the feed you want.

Is there a free esports API?

Yes, with limits. PandaScore's free plan allows 1000 requests per hour for schedules and results, OpenDota is open source for Dota 2, and STRATZ is free to use with a GraphQL API. Riot issues developer keys through an application process.

Can I use these APIs for betting?

Not all of them. PandaScore forbids betting-related usage on its stats plans. GRID, Oddin.gg, OddsMatrix and Sportradar are aimed at operators with odds and settlement.

Confirm licensing in writing, as rules vary by title and territory.

How much do esports data feeds cost?

Only PandaScore publishes rates: stats plans from 400 euros and 1000 euros per game per month, with a 10% annual discount. The rest quote privately. Budget for a sales cycle and ask whether prices depend on titles, request volume or client type.

What delivery formats do esports APIs use?

GameScorekeeper documents a REST API, STRATZ a GraphQL API and OddsMatrix offers HTTP pull and TCP socket push. Widgets and white-label sites are available from GRID and GameScorekeeper for teams that prefer embeds.

Do I need Steamworks for esports data?

Rarely. Steamworks is Valve's toolset for configuring and running games on Steam, not an esports statistics feed. Use it if you publish a game on Steam; use a data vendor for tournament and match feeds.
