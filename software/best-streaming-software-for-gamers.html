What are streaming software for gamers?

Streaming software for gamers is the program that captures a game, a webcam and a microphone, combines them into scenes, and sends the result to Twitch, YouTube or another service as a live video feed. It also records locally, and many tools add overlays, alerts or multistreaming on top.

How we ranked these

Choosing broadcasting software looks easy until the first stream: the wrong pick means a logo burned into your video, a resolution cap, a trial that ends mid-season, or an app that only runs on an operating system you do not use. This ranking covers ten tools that a PC gamer might realistically open before going live.

We read each vendor's homepage, pricing page and download page, compared the published price, platform list and limits, and weighed how well each fits someone streaming a game to Twitch or YouTube from a single PC. We did not run benchmarks, so nothing here claims frame rates or encoder quality.

Where a vendor does not publish a figure, we say so instead of guessing. Order reflects fit for a solo or small-team gaming channel, not company size.

What it costs once you outgrow the free tier

Several tools here start at zero and then gate resolution, scene count, stream length or branding behind a paid plan. Read the limits before you pick, because a 720p cap or a visible logo changes how a channel looks. One vendor charges per month from the start, and two sell on a trial.

Compare the cheapest paid step against what you would actually unlock, not the headline price.

Which operating system and hardware it assumes

A gaming rig usually runs Windows, but not always. Some programs are Windows only, some cover Windows and macOS, one is open source across three systems, and one runs entirely in a browser while a cloud service captures the console. A graphics-card app only helps if you own that vendor's card.

Check the platform line before anything else; it removes options quickly.

How much work a first stream takes

Scene building, audio routing and encoder settings are where new streamers lose an evening. Tools with templates, an overlay gallery or a built-in layout helper cut that time, while mixer-style production software expects you to know broadcast vocabulary. We weighed what each vendor says about layouts, templates and bundled alerts, since those are the parts that decide the first hour.

Reach beyond a single destination

Streaming to Twitch and YouTube together, or adding a vertical feed, is common now. Some products bundle multistreaming, some route it through a cloud service, and some leave it to a plugin or a companion app. Count how many destinations the free tier allows and whether sending extra feeds costs more, because that is where small channels hit a paywall.

    OBS Studio

    Free open source recorder and streaming app

    Why #1: Free, open source and available for Windows, macOS and Linux, which makes it the safest default for a gamer who wants no recurring cost.

    Best for
    Free setup on any desktop OS
    Pricing
    Free and open source software.
    Free option
    Open source
    Company
    OBS Studio
    Platforms
    Windows 10 and 11, macOS 13 or newer, Linux
    Key features
    Scenes with multiple sources, Window capture, Browser sources, Capture cards, H264/AAC encoding

    Nothing about the price or platform list is hard to beat: the vendor describes OBS as free and open source software for recording and live streaming, with scenes built from window captures, browser windows, webcams and capture cards. That covers the usual gamer stack of game, camera and chat window.

    It supports Windows 10 and 11, macOS 13 or newer and Linux, so a platform change later does not force a new tool. For a solo Twitch or YouTube channel it is the baseline everything else gets measured against.

    Where it falls short

    OBS ships as a blank canvas, so you build scenes, audio routing and encoder settings yourself. The pages we read mention no bundled alerts, templates or hosted chat tools, and there is no vendor support desk listed on the homepage.

    Skip it if

    Skip it if you want alerts, tipping and templates in one install, or a guided layout on day one. Streamlabs Desktop or XSplit Broadcaster come with more handholding.

What this list shows

Counting the ten records, five publish a price on the page we opened (Meld, XSplit, Lightstream, vMix and Wirecast), one is free and open source, and three give no amount at all: Streamlabs Desktop, StreamElements and the NVIDIA App. Five offer a usable free tier or free software, two lean on trials, and vMix starts at $50 per month. For a hobby channel, the free group covers the job; the paid products target people who produce shows with several cameras and guests.

Platform choice narrows the list faster than price does. Two tools run on Windows only, one covers Mac and Windows, Prism lists Windows and Apple Silicon Macs, and OBS spans three systems. Lightstream is the outlier: it runs in a browser and can capture Xbox and PlayStation in the cloud, which matters if the game is not on your PC.

The trade-off is a free tier limited to 720p, so console streamers pay for resolution.

How to choose

1. Start from your operating system

Write down whether you stream from Windows, a Mac or a console, then drop every tool that does not run there. That alone removes two or three names. If you play on a console, only the browser-based option handles it without a capture card, and if you use a Mac, XSplit and vMix are out.

Everything else is a question of price and taste.

2. Match the free tier to your destinations

List the places you will stream to this month. If the answer is one platform at 1080p, free software is enough and you should spend nothing. If you want several destinations at once, check how each vendor handles the second feed, since some include it and some route it through a paid cloud plan.

Pick the tool whose free limits do not block your plan.

3. Try one scene before you commit

Download or open your top two, build a single scene with game capture, webcam and microphone, and go through the settings screens. How quickly you reach a working scene tells you more than any feature list. Keep the second choice installed for a week as a fallback; switching between free tools costs only the time it takes to rebuild your scenes, so the risk of trying both is small.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best streaming software for gamers in 2026?

OBS Studio is our first pick for most PC gamers: it is free, open source and runs on Windows, macOS and Linux. Streamlabs Desktop is better if you want alerts and overlays bundled in, and XSplit Broadcaster is better on Windows if you prefer a guided layout. For console streams without a capture card, Lightstream is the better fit.

Is OBS Studio really free to use?

Yes. The OBS project describes the software as free and open source, and its homepage lists no paid tier. It supports Windows 10 and 11, macOS 13 or newer and Linux.

The cost is your own setup time, since scenes, audio and encoder settings are yours to configure.

Can I stream from a console without a capture card?

Lightstream says it offers cloud capture for Xbox and PlayStation and runs in the browser with nothing to download. Its free tier is limited to 720p, a 10 hour limit per stream and three scenes, with a Lightstream logo on the video, so the paid plan matters for a clean 1080p channel.

Which streaming programs run on a Mac?

OBS Studio supports macOS 13 or newer, Wirecast lists Mac and Windows, and Prism Live Studio lists macOS on Apple Silicon, Monterey 12.3 or higher. Meld Studio and Lightstream are reached through a desktop download and a browser respectively. XSplit Broadcaster and vMix are Windows applications, so skip them on a Mac.

Do I need paid software to stream at 1080p?

Not always. OBS Studio and Meld Studio are free, and Meld lists a free plan at $0 per month. Lightstream limits its free tier to 720p, while XSplit says Premium adds higher-resolution output and commercial use.

Check the exact limits of any free plan before you rely on it for a monetized channel.

Is the NVIDIA App a replacement for streaming software?

Not on its own. NVIDIA describes the app as a companion for gamers and creators that keeps drivers current, and its download page lists ShadowPlay and a Broadcast App among its features. It needs an NVIDIA GPU, and it does not replace a scene-based program for overlays, webcams and multiple sources.

When is vMix or Wirecast worth the money?

When you run a produced show rather than a solo channel. vMix is a Windows video mixer from $50 per month with a 60-day trial, and Wirecast starts at $34 per month billed yearly with unlimited cameras and remote guests. A single-camera gaming stream rarely uses those features.
