What are 3D game engines for small studios?

A 3D game engine is a toolkit for building games with depth: it renders scenes, handles physics, animation and audio, and exports to devices. For small studios, the relevant questions are cost, royalty and source access. This ranking covers engines for desktop, console and mobile, and web 3D tools, using terms published by each vendor.

How we ranked these

A team of two to thirty usually cannot absorb a licensing surprise. A fee that triggers at launch, a royalty that scales with sales, or terms that live on a page nobody read can change the budget of a project that took two years.

For this ranking we opened vendor homepages and read the statements about price, license, source access and platforms for twelve engines and frameworks. We did not run performance benchmarks or build sample games.

Terms come first in the order, followed by editor completeness and the range of devices. An engine that publishes a royalty or a free license plainly gains ground on one that links to a plans page with no figures.

Where we found nothing, the entry says that nothing is published.

Royalty and fee structure

Money terms decide whether a small studio can plan. CRYENGINE publishes a 5% royalty with the first $5K of annual revenue per project exempt. Bevy states no contracts, license fees or sales cuts.

Godot is described as free and open source. Unity and Flax Engine point to pages where terms live, and Needle Engine requires Go Pro for commercial work.

We marked each entry by what the vendor's own text says, and recorded the absence of a figure as a risk, not a neutral fact.

Source access

Reading the engine's code lets a small team fix a blocker without waiting for a vendor. Flax Engine and CRYENGINE advertise full source code, Stride states MIT licensed editor and runtime source, Bevy is MIT or Apache 2.0, and Godot is open source. Web libraries such as Three.js and Babylon.js link to public repositories.

We did not read the repositories, so license names appear only where the vendor page shows them.

Editor and artist workflow

Two programmers can live in code, but a studio with artists needs scene editing, lighting and animation tools. Unity, Godot, Flax, Stride and CRYENGINE ship editors; Bevy is code-first; Wonderland Engine and Needle Engine add web-focused tooling. The question to ask is who on the team will touch the engine daily: if the answer includes non-programmers, a full editor outranks a clean license.

Platform coverage

Where you can publish limits where you can earn. Unity lists more than 25 platforms, CRYENGINE names PC, Xbox, PlayStation and Oculus, Bevy lists desktop, web, iOS and Android, and Stride lists desktop and VR. Web engines reach any browser but not stores.

Several vendors state no platform list at all, so we treated those as unconfirmed.

    Godot

    Free engine with open source and node-based scenes

    Why #2: No fee, readable source and a foundation behind it, which makes the cost side of a small-studio plan very easy.

    Best for
    Small teams that refuse engine fees entirely
    Pricing
    The vendor calls it free and open source and states nothing else about price; the license is not named in the page text.
    Free option
    Open source
    Company
    Godot
    Platforms
    Desktop, mobile and web exports; consoles via third-party publishers
    Key features
    Node and Scene system, Simple 3D, OpenXR and WebXR, GDScript, C#, C++

    Godot answers the licensing question by making it irrelevant. The homepage calls it a free, open-source 2D and 3D game engine, with a node and scene system and scripting in GDScript, C#, C++ or GDExtension.

    The 3D side is described as simple, and OpenXR and WebXR support is listed for headset projects. Juan Linietsky and Ariel Manzur are named as creators, and the project is hosted by the Godot Foundation.

    Patron and corporate sponsors named include Scorewarrior, W4 Games, JetBrains and Mega Crit. Version 4.7.2 is the current download shown.

    Where it falls short

    The homepage describes its 3D as simple, which is an honest limit for studios chasing film-grade visuals. Console release depends on third-party publishers.

    The only editor download link on the homepage is for Windows, and C# on the web needs Godot 3.

    Skip it if

    Teams building a visually heavy 3D title with console targets from day one should compare Unity or CryEngine.

    Bevy is for studios whose engineers already write Rust. The homepage calls it a refreshingly simple data-driven engine, dual-licensed under MIT or Apache 2.0, with no contracts, license fees or sales cuts.

What this list shows

The facts split the field cleanly. Four of the twelve state permissive open-source terms or a plain free model (Godot, Bevy, Stride and O3DE), two name a fee structure for commercial use (CRYENGINE with a 5% royalty and Needle Engine with Go Pro), and five publish no pricing or license text on the homepage at all (Unity, Flax, Fyrox, Babylon.js and Three.js). Wonderland Engine offers a free start with no plan figures.

Four of the twelve target the browser.

The trade-off is predictability against polish. Established engines offer wider tooling and platform lists but price pages that sit one click away, while open engines give certainty on cost and ask for more engineering. Web 3D tools suit configurators and browser games, yet they do not publish store builds.

For a studio with two programmers, source access and a clear license reduce risk more than extra features do.

How to choose

1. Run the royalty math at three revenue levels

Take a conservative, a likely and a hopeful sales figure and apply each engine's published terms. For CRYENGINE, subtract the first $5K per project and apply 5%. For Bevy, Godot and Stride the result is zero engine cost.

For Unity and Flax, open the plan or licensing page and write the figures down with today's date before you decide.

2. Name the person who will open the engine daily

If artists and designers will work in the tool every day, shortlist the engines with full editors and test one real scene in each. If only engineers will touch it, code-first choices like Bevy become reasonable. Count the team in months of onboarding, not in features, because a language switch from C# to Rust costs more than a license.

3. Match platforms to your first publisher deal

Write down which stores and devices your plan names, then compare the vendor lists. Mobile and consoles narrow the field to Unity and a few others; a browser product opens the web-focused group. If a vendor lists no platforms, ask for a written confirmation before you plan around it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best 3D game engine for a small studio in 2026?

Unity ranks first for platform reach, with more than 25 platforms listed, but its homepage shows no licensing figures. Godot is better when a zero-fee, open-source engine matters, and Flax Engine is better when a team wants engine source in C++ or C#. CRYENGINE is the pick when a published royalty is required.

Which 3D engine has no royalties?

Bevy states no contracts, license fees or sales cuts, Godot is described as free and open source, and Stride's editor and runtime are MIT licensed. CRYENGINE does charge a 5% royalty after the first $5K of annual revenue per project, so it is the opposite case.

How much does CRYENGINE cost?

The homepage says it is free to use, with a 5% royalty when you ship and the first $5K of annual revenue per project royalty-free. Full source code access is listed. Confirm current terms on the vendor's own page before signing a budget.

Can a team of five use Rust for 3D games?

Yes, through Bevy or Fyrox, both built in Rust. Bevy lists 2D and 3D renderers, a render graph and desktop, web and mobile targets under MIT or Apache 2.0. Fyrox publishes few facts on its homepage, so verify its license and platforms yourself.

Is Godot good enough for 3D?

The homepage describes its 3D as simple, and lists OpenXR and WebXR support. For many small-team projects that is enough, and the cost is zero. If you need heavy visual fidelity or direct console support, compare Unity and CRYENGINE; Godot sends consoles through third-party publishers.

Which engine suits 3D on the web?

Babylon.js, Wonderland Engine, Needle Engine and Three.js all target the browser. Wonderland Engine adds a visual editor and WebXR, Needle Engine exports from Unity or Blender, and Babylon.js lists WebGPU. Needle needs Go Pro for commercial use; the others publish no price in the page text.

What does open source mean for a studio's risk?

It lowers fee risk and lets you patch the engine, but it moves support onto your team. Godot is hosted by the Godot Foundation, O3DE by the Open 3D Foundation, and Stride is backed by the .NET Foundation. Check the named license, because some homepages do not state it.
