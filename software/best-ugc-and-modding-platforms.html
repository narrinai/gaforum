What are UGC and modding platforms?

A UGC and modding platform is a service that stores, distributes and curates content players make for a game, such as mods, maps, skins and whole experiences. Studios either plug one into their own game through an API or SDK, or rely on a public hub where the community already uploads.

How we ranked these

A studio that wants mods has two separate problems. One is plumbing: where files live, how a player subscribes to them, and how they reach consoles and mobile.

The other is people: who reviews uploads, who pays creators and who answers when a mod breaks the game. The services below solve different halves of that pair, and several are not built for studios at all.

This ranking weighs the eleven platforms for a small or mid-size studio shipping its own game. We read vendor home pages, About pages, legal notices and pricing pages, compared what each says about integration, monetization and moderation, and checked what each page leaves unsaid.

Where a vendor publishes no price, we say so instead of guessing. Vendor figures such as download counts are marked as claims.

Integration path into your own game

Some services give studios an API, an SDK and engine plugins, so the mod browser lives inside the game. Others are public sites where players upload files and a separate manager installs them. Check which one a platform offers before anything else, because it decides how much engineering you owe.

In-game browsing also removes the step where players leave the game to hunt for files, which is where most casual players give up on mods.

Reach across PC, console and mobile

A hub that only serves Windows players limits a console release, and a console-first catalog does little for a PC strategy game. Look for what each vendor states about supported device families, and whether a title can ship the same mod set to all of them. Several listed services are tied to one game or one store, which is fine when your game matches and a dead end when it does not.

Creator payouts and revenue paths

Creators stay longer when they can earn something. The options in this list range from ad revenue on project pages, to storefront cuts, to a stated refusal to sell mods at all. Decide whether you want paid mods, a donation model or free content only, then pick a platform whose terms agree with you.

Payment liability matters too, since someone has to handle tax and chargebacks.

Moderation and legal exposure

Hosting other people's files brings copyright claims, malicious uploads and offensive content. Read what each vendor says about rules, copyright policies and who reviews submissions: staff, volunteers or the studio itself. A platform with volunteer moderators can be a good community home yet offers weaker guarantees than a contract with a commercial operator.

Studios with children in their audience should weigh this above price.

What this list shows

Counting the eleven entries, none shows a studio price in the sources we opened. CurseForge offers a free client and GameBanana and ModWorkshop show no paywalls, but every studio-facing integration either hides its amounts or sells through a contact form, so budgeting for a mod platform usually starts with an email. Several services focus on a single game family (Minecraft, PAYDAY or Steam titles), while others such as Thunderstore and mod.io aim at many games.

Only ModWorkshop states a country through its legal notice, the Netherlands.

The trade-off in this niche is control against audience. A studio-integrated service such as mod.io gives you an in-game browser and a branded experience, but you start with an empty catalog. A public hub arrives with a ready audience of modders and players, yet your game shares the stage with every other title and your brand has little say.

Several hubs also run on ads or creator programs that you cannot configure, so moderation and monetization policy belongs to the vendor, not to you.

How to choose

1. Decide whether mods live inside your game

If players should browse and install mods without leaving the title, you need a platform with an API, SDK or engine plugin, and you should plan engineering time for it. If the community already uses a public hub for your genre, linking out may be enough. Write down which one you mean before comparing vendors, because the two groups on this list barely overlap.

2. Match platforms to the devices you ship on

List every device your game launches on and check each vendor's stated coverage line by line. A Windows-only manager does nothing for console players, and a Steam-only workshop does nothing for a release on another store. If a vendor page does not name a platform, ask in writing before you build around it.

3. Pin down money and moderation in writing

Ask each shortlisted vendor who handles payouts, who reviews uploads, and what happens to a mod that breaks copyright rules. For paid mods, confirm who is merchant of record. For free hubs, read the rules page and the copyright policy.

Keep the answers with your contract notes so the support team can point to them later.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best UGC and modding platform in 2026?

For a studio that wants mods inside its own game across PC, console and mobile, mod.io ranks first because it offers an API, an SDK and Unity and Unreal plugins. CurseForge is the better pick when you want an existing modder audience and creator payouts, and Steam Workshop fits a game that ships on Steam only.

Which platform lets creators earn money from their mods?

Per the vendors' own pages, CurseForge states it has paid creators more than $33.5 million, Modrinth says creators can earn ad revenue from project pages, and Overwolf mentions a $50 million creator fund. GameBanana takes the opposite stance and says it never sells mods. Check payout terms before you commit.

Can I add mod support to a console game?

mod.io says it supports mods on PC, console and mobile, and CurseForge says it distributes to PC, console and mobile for studios. Steam Workshop is a Steam feature and does not describe console distribution. Confirm the exact console storefronts with the vendor, since the pages we read do not list them.

Do I need a public mod site if my game already has an in-game browser?

Often yes, because modders gather where tutorials, forums and file hosting already exist. An in-game browser handles install and updates, while a public site handles discovery and community. Some studios use both; ModWorkshop, for example, built an official in-game mod browser for PAYDAY 2 on top of its site.

How do these platforms handle moderation?

It varies. ModWorkshop says its moderators are volunteers, Modrinth publishes content rules and a copyright policy, and Roblox's pages we opened describe no moderation process. Most pages do not describe automated scanning.

If your audience includes minors, add a dedicated safety tool from our trust and safety ranking.

Is any of this free for studios?

CurseForge says its client is free to download, ModWorkshop and GameBanana show no paywalls, and Thunderstore shows no prices. mod.io, Overwolf and Steam Workshop publish no pricing on the pages we opened, so costs for studio integrations are unknown until you ask.

What should a small studio budget for mod support?

Engineering time usually costs more than the platform. Plan for SDK integration, a review process and support for broken mods. Since most vendors here publish no prices, request a written quote and ask whether revenue share applies before you design your roadmap around mods.
