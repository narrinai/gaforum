What are live streaming APIs and SDKs?

A live streaming API is a hosted service that ingests video from an encoder or device, converts it, and delivers it to viewers through code you control. SDKs wrap the same service for web, iOS and Android. They let a product team add live video without building servers, encoders and global delivery themselves.

How we ranked these

Developers shopping for live video face two different products wearing the same label. One broadcasts a feed from a stage to a large audience; the other connects people in a room who talk to each other.

Pricing, latency and SDK design differ between the two, and so does the bill when your audience grows. This ranking is written for engineering leads and product managers choosing a vendor for an app, a game site or an esports platform.

We read each vendor's homepage, product and pricing pages, compared the published rates, and noted what is missing. We ran no load tests and make no latency claims beyond what vendors state.

Thirteen services are ranked, with published pricing and live broadcast focus weighted most.

Broadcast or conversation

Decide first whether viewers watch or talk. Services such as Mux and Cloudflare Stream accept RTMP or SRT and fan out HLS to many viewers. Services such as Twilio Video, Daily and Agora are built for participants who send video too.

A few, including Amazon IVS and LiveKit, cover both. We ranked broadcast-capable services higher here because the category is live streaming, and noted where a product is call-first.

Rate card you can model

A usage bill is a spreadsheet exercise only if the rates are public. Mux, api.video, Amazon IVS and Twilio print per-minute or per-hour figures. Others link to a pricing page or ask for contact.

We favored vendors whose numbers appear in the pages we read, and we flagged the units, because per-participant, per-minute and per-hour billing scale very differently as audiences grow.

Ingest and output protocols

Your encoder or app dictates the entry protocol, and your viewers' devices dictate the output. Cloudflare Stream lists RTMP and SRT ingest with HLS and DASH output. Many broadcast services accept RTMP, while WebRTC ingest is rarer and worth checking.

Check that the service takes what your stack sends and plays where your audience watches, before judging features around the edges.

Open source and exit options

Video infrastructure is hard to leave once your player and ingest are wired in. LiveKit is open source and can be self-hosted; Daily maintains the open source Pipecat project. Hosted-only services trade that portability for convenience.

We noted open components where the vendors state them, since they give a way out if prices or terms change.

What this list shows

The rate cards in this list barely compare. Amazon IVS bills by channel hour, Mux by encoding and delivery minute, api.video by minutes stored and delivered, Twilio by participant minute, and Ceeblue by monthly package in euros. Five of thirteen services state concrete amounts on the pages we read: Mux, Amazon IVS, api.video, Twilio and Ceeblue.

Agora, Daily, 100ms, Cloudflare Stream and Vonage state a free start or link to a pricing page, and Dolby OptiView, LiveKit's paid tiers and Livepeer give no customer rates at all.

Free entry is common: Mux, Amazon IVS, api.video, Agora, LiveKit, Daily and Twilio each offer a free tier, credit or trial on the pages we read. Only one, api.video, shows a France address on its homepage, and Ceeblue lists offices in the Netherlands, Germany and Spain; the rest give no location in what we read. The split to remember is that call-first SDKs are cheaper to start with but expensive per large audience, while broadcast services price delivery minutes.

How to choose

1. Sketch the audience shape on paper

Count how many people send video and how many only watch. If the answer is a handful and thousands, shortlist Mux, Amazon IVS or Cloudflare Stream. If everyone is both, shortlist LiveKit, Daily or Agora.

Writing the shape down before comparing prices prevents choosing a call tool for a broadcast and discovering the per-participant bill later.

2. Model one month of real usage

Take your expected viewer minutes, stream hours and storage, and run them through the published rates of your top three. Mux and Amazon IVS give enough numbers to do this today. For vendors that do not publish rates, ask for a quote against the same sheet.

A realistic month of use shows which services stay affordable when the audience doubles.

3. Build a one-day prototype before committing

Most services offer a free tier or trial. Wire an encoder or camera app into two candidates, stream for an hour, and watch on the devices your viewers use. Note delay, setup effort and documentation clarity.

Check what happens after the free period ends, such as api.video's sandbox deleting content after 24 hours, so a prototype does not become a trap.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best live streaming API in 2026?

Mux is our top pick because it publishes live encoding, storage and delivery rates and bundles a player and analytics. Amazon IVS is better if your stack is already on AWS and you need built-in chat. api.video is a good third option for teams that want free encoding and simple hosting rates.

What is the difference between a streaming API and a WebRTC SDK?

A streaming API takes a feed from an encoder and delivers it to many viewers, usually over HLS. A WebRTC SDK connects participants in rooms with very low delay. Mux and Cloudflare Stream are streaming APIs; Twilio Video, Daily and Agora lean toward WebRTC.

LiveKit and Amazon IVS cover real-time as well.

Which live streaming APIs have a free tier?

Mux is free to start with a monthly usage credit, Amazon IVS has a first-12-months free tier, api.video has a limited sandbox, Agora gives 10,000 minutes, LiveKit gives 1,000 agent session minutes, and Twilio offers a trial without a credit card. Limits differ, so read them before building a demo on one.

Is there a European live streaming API?

api.video shows a French address on its homepage, and Ceeblue B.V. lists offices in the Netherlands, Germany and Spain. Others on this page do not state a location in what we read. If data residency matters, ask each vendor where video is processed and stored, not just where the company sits.

Can I self-host instead of paying per minute?

Yes, for some components. LiveKit is open source and Daily maintains the open source Pipecat framework. You then pay for servers and operations rather than per-minute fees, which is cheaper at high volume only if your team can run it.

For servers that accept RTMP or SRT, see our streaming media server ranking.

How much does live streaming cost per hour?

It varies by unit. Amazon IVS lists basic channel input at $0.20 per hour, and Mux lists live 1080p Plus encoding at $0.03125 per minute, which is $1.875 per hour before delivery. Twilio charges from $0.004 per participant per minute.

Add storage and delivery, then multiply by audience size.

Do I need an API if I just stream a tournament?

Usually not. A tournament stream typically goes from production software to a platform such as Twitch or YouTube. A streaming API makes sense when you embed the stream in your own site or app, or need custom chat, overlays or access control.
