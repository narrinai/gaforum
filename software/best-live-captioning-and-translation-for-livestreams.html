What are live captioning and translation tools for livestreams?

Live captioning and translation for livestreams converts spoken words into text within seconds, and can translate that text or speech into other languages using AI, human interpreters or both. The output appears as captions on the viewer's screen, a separate audio channel, or a text feed that a player can display.

How we ranked these

Captions and translation decide whether a livestream reaches everyone who registered. Viewers watch muted at work, speak other languages, or rely on text for access, and a keynote without them quietly loses part of its audience.

The tools here approach the job from three directions: turnkey interpreting and caption services, caption vendors with broadcast or accessibility roots, and speech APIs a developer wires into a player. We read each vendor's pages for product descriptions, pricing, language claims, certifications and company details, and did not test accuracy or latency ourselves.

Language counts and security statements are the vendors' own. Ranking favors tools that document live use and language coverage, then those with clear prices, with turnkey services above building blocks for event teams without engineers.

Human interpreters or machine only

AI translation is quick and cheap, and human simultaneous interpretation handles nuance, humor and regulated content. Interprefy and KUDO offer both; Wordly describes an AI-only service; Rev lists human captions in English and Spanish. Decide by the stakes: a gaming community stream may accept AI, while a shareholder or legal broadcast may need a person on the line.

Language coverage and where output appears

KUDO states 200 languages on any device or platform. Other vendors give no count on the pages we read. Check whether translation arrives as captions inside the stream, as a separate audio channel, or in a listener app, since Interprefy offers a web platform and a mobile app, and Wordly works inside Zoom.

Match the delivery method to how viewers watch.

Fit with your streaming stack

Turnkey services plug into meeting and event platforms; speech APIs need engineering. Speechmatics, Deepgram and AssemblyAI give you transcription to build around, while Wordly's Zoom integration and KUDO's meeting-platform integrations ask for less work. Also consider caption embedding in a broadcast chain, where Ai-Media's encoder heritage is relevant.

Price visibility and trial credits

Speechmatics lists $0.12 per hour with $100 of free credit, Deepgram a $200 credit and prepaid plans from $4,000 a year, and Otter plans from free to $30. The turnkey services mostly ask for a quote. Request prices per event hour and per language, and ask what a second language or a human interpreter adds.

What this list shows

Eleven tools, three kinds of vendor. Four deliver event translation and live captions as a service (Interprefy, KUDO, Wordly, Ai-Media), three sell captioning and transcription with accessibility or legal roots (Verbit, 3Play Media, Rev), three are speech APIs (Speechmatics, Deepgram, AssemblyAI) and one is a meeting notetaker (Otter). Only Speechmatics, Deepgram and Otter print dollar figures on the pages we read, and four tools offer free credits, a free plan or a free API trial: Speechmatics, Deepgram, AssemblyAI and Otter.

The trade-off is effort against control. APIs are cheap per hour and flexible, but your engineers own latency, display and language handling. Services take that work away and add interpreters, at a price you learn only on a sales call.

We left out Microsoft Teams live events because Microsoft Learn says that format retired on June 30, 2026, and 3Play Media's pricing page returned a 404, so its entry rests on the homepage.

How to choose

1. Name the languages and the stakes

List the languages viewers actually use, from registration data if you have it, and decide which sessions can use machine output. A launch keynote may need a human interpreter in two languages and AI captions in ten more. Having this list ready turns vendor quotes from vague to comparable.

2. Decide where captions will appear

Embedded captions in the video, a separate caption feed for the player, a listener app or Zoom's built-in translation each require different setup. Ask each vendor how output reaches your platform and what delay to expect. Test with the real stream key and the real speakers, because accents and product names are where errors cluster.

3. Price per event hour and plan the archive

Ask for a rate per hour per language, and what rehearsals cost. Then decide how the recording gets proper subtitles afterward: Rev, Verbit and 3Play Media focus on recorded transcripts and captions, which can polish the on-demand version after the live event. Keep a custom vocabulary list for names and game terms in every case.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best live captioning and translation tool for livestreams in 2026?

Interprefy is our first pick for multilingual events because it offers human simultaneous interpretation, AI translation and live captions with stated ISO 27001 certification. KUDO is better when you need the widest language range (200, per the vendor), and Wordly when the stream already runs in Zoom.

Are AI captions accurate enough for a livestream?

Often for clear speech, less so with accents, jargon or crosstalk. We did not measure accuracy and vendors give no figures on the pages we read. Test with your own speakers and product names, and use a human service for high-stakes events.

How much does live translation cost?

Event services such as Interprefy, KUDO and Wordly publish no prices on the pages we read. Speech APIs do: Speechmatics from $0.12 per hour and Deepgram with a $200 credit. Human interpreters add cost, so ask for a rate per hour per language.

Do I need a developer to add captions?

Not for turnkey services; KUDO and Wordly integrate with meeting platforms. For Speechmatics, Deepgram or AssemblyAI, yes: you receive transcription through an API and must build the caption display and delay handling.

Can I get captions for a stream that is not on Zoom?

Yes. KUDO states it works on any device or platform, Interprefy offers a web platform and mobile app, and the APIs accept audio from any source. Wordly's Zoom integration is the most platform-specific option in the list.

What about captions for the recording afterward?

Rev lists human captions in English and Spanish, Verbit lists captioning, dubbing and translation, and 3Play Media lists captioning and localization. These suit the on-demand version, where you can fix names and timing before publishing.

Is Otter good for livestream captions?

It is good for meeting transcripts: the free plan gives 300 monthly minutes and it works with Zoom, Teams and Google Meet. The pages we read do not describe translation for viewers, so it fits internal notes better than public captions.
